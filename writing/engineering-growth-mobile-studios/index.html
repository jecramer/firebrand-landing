Engineering Growth: How Mobile Studios Can Build Games That Keep Growing

Building the organisational, technical and operational systems required to sustain growth in mature live games.

Creativity and execution are a core strength of Western mobile free to play studios, yet many still lag behind in positioning growth strategy as their key competitive advantage.

Over indexing on brand and aligning studio operations around creative output and narrative elements rather than growth is a structural disadvantage, especially when compared to Eastern studios who construct growth levers into every facet of live game production. The western approach is to leave success to market forces and chance, while the Chinese and Turkish are engineering growth into every conversion point. This essay outlines an actionable approach to enable Western game studios to position themselves for competitive growth and reshape what the next decade of mobile F2P development could look like.

Introduction

Recently, a mobile game studio CEO replied to a pitch I sent with a line that captures the dominant Western mobile F2P mindset, saying they believed in their product but were relying on their own resources until the product proved itself. I happened to know that the studio is working on a new game and wanted to offer consultation to help with their decision making process - specifically around building for growth. The response is honest and the position is widely held. Taking such a passive approach is also the wrong posturing.

Embedded in it is the assumption that the success of a game is something external that can only be uncovered through market response rather than engineered into the studio’s core operating model. The implicit theory is that growth is what happens when a good product meets a receptive market at exactly the right time.

The studio’s job is to make the product, while the market does the rest. In other words: luck.

This framing is entirely wrong, and explains a great deal about why Western mobile F2P studios are losing ground to Eastern operators who have come to dominate the global revenue charts, and who show no signs of slowing down.

Growth is not an outcome that follows product quality when the stars align, it’s a discipline that gets engineered into the organisation, which then builds the products that grow systematically. The studios that have understood this are producing dominant live service titles. The ones that haven’t are producing competent games whose revenue plateaus a year post-launch and never recovers.

The cleanest current proof of this is Century Games. Whiteout Survival is generating growth that very few Western companies are able to match, and new title Tasty Travels is on a similar trajectory.

While the product quality and game polish is high, that alone is not a satisfactory explanation for their continued success. The explanation is both technical and operational. Looking from the outside, it is reasonable to infer that every conversion touchpoint in their games has its own dedicated team, with the authority and the infrastructure to run continuous experiments to improve that touchpoint. The org chart is therefore built around the levers that move revenue, and the product is the surface on which those levers operate.

The cycle of analyse → design → deploy → measure → iterate runs without huge bureaucratic interruption because the organisational structure is designed to make it run.

Western studios approach this very differently. The traditional Western studio model often places growth (UA, marketing) adjacent to the product rather than embedded inside it and treats the core game team as an immovable monolithic structure that can never be torn down and rethought.

Within this structure, the whole game team is nominally responsible for all conversion touchpoints, which in practice means that no single function has the mandate, the capacity, or the authority to drive sustained growth across them. Product teams build features. UA acquires the players. LiveOps runs events. Tech supports everyone. These teams meet often to coordinate, but each operates against its own roadmap and incentives. The result is a competent live service that generates predictable revenue and, concurrently, is structurally and operationally incapable of compounding growth.

This essay makes the case that growth in a mature mobile F2P game should be a higher organisational function with an explicit mandate that unifies product, design, liveops, UA, community, and player experience; supported by dedicated data and engineering capacity; and operating with the mandate and technical ability to make specific product changes outside of main feature development cycles.

The work of this function is not to replace or dilute existing teams. It is to create the synapses that reinforce the studio’s existing capabilities and realign the organisation as a whole from passive and at the mercy of market forces, to an assertive and deliberate growth machine. It does this by establishing a common growth thesis rather than around siloed departmental control over the product.

The studios that restructure around this principle will define what the next decade of Western mobile F2P looks like. The studios that don’t will continue to manage decline while watching Century Games and others expand into every category the West used to own.

Engineered Growth Models, A Definition

The structural gap between Eastern and Western mobile F2P studios is observable in operational terms and market signals long before it shows up in revenue terms. Typically, revenue is a lagging indicator; analysing it makes for good podcast and blog content but the decisions that led to those results were made months, if not years earlier and make for much less exciting content.

Rather, leading indicators of success are observable in everything from technical aspects of the game’s client, the ratio of reviews:downloads, market whitespace, content production, release cadence, and many other data points.

A title with engineered growth presents in specific ways. The binary download is small, typically under 150MB, with an OTA content update on first load or after FTUE completion. Bundles and popups feel targeted and arrive at non-specific times, suggesting cohort-based triggers rather than scripted beats. The ad libraries show continuous creative variation. Store positioning is updated frequently. Event cadence feels more dynamic and tailored to cohort behaviour, with overlapping windows aimed at different segments. Feature releases respond visibly to player behaviour rather than to a fixed production calendar.

A title without engineered growth presents differently. The binary on the store is often heavier and more of the game ships inside it. The ad library shows fewer variants and longer-lived assets. Events align to the monthly binary release. Store positioning is largely static. Bundles and popups arrive at scripted moments and look and feel static. Feature releases follow a roadmap decided in advance and executed against regardless of what the data shows.

Phases of Growth

Growth in the soft/post-launch phase of a mobile F2P game with reasonable performance metrics is a relatively easy challenge for most teams.

At launch, the scope of the product is limited, the golden cohort is not yet exhausted, and the work is largely about finding the players who will respond to the game as-built and acquiring them cheaply and efficiently. Performance marketing carries most of the load. The growth function, where it exists as a named role, is essentially a UA function with a broader title. The product team stays focused on feature completeness and deepening the experience to optimise for long term engagement and retention.

Growth in a game’s mature phase is a different challenge entirely, and it is the problem most Western studios are currently failing to solve. The audience installing the game now is no longer the audience the game was built for.

The high-value cohorts from the post-launch phase have shifted in their motivations or moved on to competitor titles. The game’s economy has accumulated hoarders and experienced cohorts who know how to min-max their progress, avoiding monetisation pinch points. The LiveOps calendar has become a treadmill that produces predictable engagement without producing new revenue. The creative library is cycling through variants of campaigns that worked twelve months ago and no longer do.

Operational Foundation & Transition

Team structures in this phase, for most projects, remain locked long after the market conditions have changed, however. More often than not, product responsibility gets locked into a hierarchical structure that negatively affects game performance and leaves revenue in ever decreasing circles.

Writing purely from experience, once a game in its early phase has generated significant revenues, the leads of that product often feel entitled to maintain the team structure that generated early success and therefore retain ultimate control over the game’s roadmap and team composition. This is a pattern I have seen repeated multiple times across multiple games in multiple studios. Sometimes it is driven by ego, but mostly the cause is a result of a vacuum at the CPO/ Growth level arguing that the game’s growth phase has shifted and a new structure is urgently needed.

Solving this requires a set of capabilities that do not sit cleanly within any existing functional team.

Six Capabilities Conventional Operational Structures Cannot Deliver

Audience re-identification. The personas the studio worked with at launch are no longer accurate. The work is to re-segment the current playerbase by behavioural and monetisation patterns, identify which cohorts are actually driving revenue today, and understand what those cohorts want from the game now. This requires data analysis that crosses product, UA, and liveops boundaries and is rarely owned by any of them as a continuous function.

Multivariate testing at high velocity. A studio that can only test one variant per release cycle is gambling on quarterly intervals. Mature-phase growth requires the capacity to run several concurrent experiments against different cohorts, with results readable within days rather than weeks, and with the technical infrastructure to act on those results immediately. Most Western studios have neither the infrastructure nor the team structure to operate at this tempo.

Cross-functional leverage point identification. Product improvements and LiveOps events that produce growth in the mature phase are rarely contained within one function. A high-spending cohort that has stopped converting on a specific bundle category is not a UA problem, a product problem, or a LiveOps problem; it is all three. Finding the solution requires someone whose job is to look across the functions and identify where the conversion leverage actually exists and take action on it. Few existing functional roles in Western studios have this scope by default.

Integrated campaigns connecting economy, content, and creative. A campaign that identifies targets a specific cohort, releases content designed for them, sinks an economy item they have hoarded, and runs ad creative that mirrors the in-game experience is one coordinated motion executed across four functions with aligned timing. Most studios run these as four separate functions that fail to converge, if they run them at all.

Market intelligence aligned with feature production. The design choices the studio makes are responses to what is happening in the broader market: which creative formats are converting, which audiences are migrating between games, which interaction patterns are gaining traction in adjacent verticals. This intelligence has to flow continuously into the design and LiveOps process, not arrive as an easily-ignored quarterly slide deck.

Decision-specific analytics. The dashboards most studios use are generic KPI walls designed to serve everyone equally and therefore serve no one well. Mature-phase growth requires analytics built around the specific decisions being made by the growth function, updated at the cadence those decisions are made, and accessible without engineering tickets.

Growth Cycle & Six-Phase Core Loop

Engineered growth is not one single operation or a one-time strategic exercise. It is a repeating cycle that connects market intelligence to player behaviour to in-game design to performance marketing, and back to analysis. The cycle has six phases. Each phase produces an output that becomes the input for the next, and the cycle re-enters phase one with refined inputs at the end of each turn.

Phase 1: Identification of an analogue growth opportunity. The cycle begins with pattern analysis across install sources, ad libraries, and adjacent market signals. The goal is to identify audiences whose fingerprint maps to the game’s current or potential player base. To give an example: the BookTok phenomenon has produced an enormous English-speaking female reading audience whose interaction patterns and content preferences map closely to certain narrative-driven mobile games. Reading apps targeting this audience run playable creatives that convert exceptionally well. Games with the right narrative foundation should tap that traffic with creatives designed against the same interaction grammar. The output is a documented opportunity: a specific cohort, a specific behavioural fingerprint, and a specific hypothesis about how the game could serve that audience.

Phase 2: Design of mechanics and content to exploit the opportunity. Once an opportunity is identified, design translates it into something native to the game. For example, a Wordle-style daily appointment mechanic that uses inventory items as the currency sink. Weekly story unlocks gated by hard currency. The design work is driven by research revealed in Phase 1. The output of this phase is a feature or event, designed against the growth hypothesis.

Phase 3: Integration into normal release cadence. The new feature is developed alongside the team’s planned feature work. It does not disrupt the feature team’s roadmap. The growth function and the GM agree on the release window, so that production planning can incorporate the work without friction. The output of this phase is a shipped binary containing the feature’s client-side components, configured to be activated remotely.

Phase 4: Remote triggering of events via configuration infrastructure. The new content goes live independently of the binary release. Server configuration starts the event, defines its window, sets the cohort targeting, and adjusts parameters during execution as needed. This is the technical foundation that makes time-bounded events possible; without it, every event requires an engineering push and the cycle slows to the cadence of the release calendar. The output of this phase is an active time limited event running according to pre-defined parameters.

Phase 5: Integrated campaign execution aligned to in-game events. Performance marketing creative is built around the same visuals, themes, and player motivations identified in phase one. The ad spend, the creative direction, the in-game event, and the bundle structure all reinforce each other, because they were designed together from the same source insight. The user who clicks the ad lands in the game and finds the experience the ad promised, configured to convert against the motivation that brought them in. The output of this phase is a coordinated acquisition and conversion motion executing against the target audience.

Phase 6: Analysis, learning, and re-entry. Performance data from the campaign and the in-game event flow back into the visualisation layer, refining the next iteration. Cohorts that responded well surface new analogue opportunities; cohorts that didn’t surface new questions. The growth operation itself produces data about player motivations, economy responses, and creative effectiveness that feeds the next pass through phase one. The output of this phase is a documented learning and a sharper version of the inputs that started the cycle.

A studio running the cycle once produces a single conversion rate improvement and a single learning. A studio running the cycle weekly produces a flywheel; each turn improves the inputs for the next. A studio running the cycle weekly across multiple conversion-point teams in parallel produces a structural advantage that compounds across the entire live product. Each team’s learnings transfer to the others through shared infrastructure and aligned cadence. The studio’s understanding of its audience deepens with every cycle; its ability to design effective features sharpens; its creative output grows more targeted. None of these gains are visible to competitors from outside the studio, because the work is invisible by design. What is visible is the cumulative output: higher event cadence, more responsive creative, faster reaction to market shifts, sustained chart positions that competitors cannot explain.

Growth as a structural moat

A competitor observing a studio operating this cycle sees only the symptoms. The events shipping weekly. The creative variants in the ad library. The store positioning updates. The retained and growing revenue quarter by quarter. None of these are themselves the advantage. The advantage is the cycle that produced them, and the cycle is not reverse-engineerable from outside. By the time a competitor identifies the pattern, the studio running the cycle is several turns ahead and operating against an information set the competitor does not have access to. This is how growth becomes a moat: not through a single insight or feature, but through a system that produces continuous insight at a tempo competitors cannot match without first building the system themselves. And building the system, as the remainder of this paper argues, requires structural decisions that most Western studios are currently unwilling or unable to make.

The Growth Cell as Operational Unit

In this Growth model, LiveOps becomes conversion point optimisation and takes a broader role than in traditional game production models. As discussed earlier, the traditional Western model treats the game production team as a monolithic structure that cannot be torn down and rethought depending on the growth phase the game is currently in. This model casts that traditional structure aside and introduces Growth Cells, which are multidisciplinary teams consisting of miniature game teams, fully capable of designing, building, and shipping everything from mini-games to feature variants to bundle redesigns, supported by their own analytics and dashboarding capacity. They run end-to-end conversion point improvement operations that are solely focused on revenue growth.

Growth cycles are determined and executed independently of the broader production team, and the cell retains a level of autonomy that allows it to run experiments and multivariate tests without external dependency.

When we talk about conversion points, we mean any measurable activity within the game that contributes directly or indirectly to monetisation. Whether this means changes to FTUE steps, UI improvements on a bundle popup, or the creation of a daily appointment mechanic targeted at installs from a specific UA campaign’s creative assets and value proposition. The goal of a Growth Cell is to focus on marginal gains that, when combined, deliver significant ongoing improvements to the product as a whole.

Each conversion point or cluster of conversion points is the responsibility of one dedicated Growth Cell. Team composition includes a strategist or growth lead, an economy designer, a data engineer, a game developer, an artist, and a floating UA manager or creative producer. Five to seven people at the maximum. This team owns the full cycle and has the executive mandate to ship according to its own cadence, in coordination with other teams to avoid overlap.

The discipline of marginal gains is the underlying principle of this structure. A single monolithic production team working on one large problem at a time can rarely produce 5 to 10 percent improvements in any individual metric, because the team’s attention is distributed across too many surfaces and the iteration cycle on any one of them is too slow. The structural improvement available within a single feature, designed in advance and shipped once, is bounded by how well the feature was specified at the start.

Growth Cells operate on the opposite principle. Each cell focuses on a single conversion point and produces continuous incremental improvements to it. A 1-2 percent uplift in FTUE completion is a small win in isolation. A 1-2 percent uplift in day-one return is another small win. A 2-3 percent uplift in first-session purchase conversion is another. None of these individually would justify the attention of a full game team. All of them executed simultaneously by dedicated cells, running in parallel and reinforcing each other, can produce a net improvement of 10 to 15 percent across the game’s overall performance. The mathematics is straightforward; small gains, applied to multiple surfaces, multiply rather than add.

This is the structural reason the model works. The improvements that engineered growth produces are rarely visible as single dramatic wins. They are visible as a steady upward drift in aggregate performance that competitors cannot easily identify the source of, because no single feature or change accounts for the result. The advantage is the cumulative output of multiple cells operating against multiple conversion points on overlapping schedules, none of them individually decisive, all of them collectively the difference between a game that plateaus and a game whose performance trajectory keeps trending upward.

Measurable Economic Advantage

The economic case for engineered growth is straightforward because Growth Cells operate against revenue surfaces that already exist at scale. Small percentage improvements against mature live products compound into disproportionately large financial outcomes.

Consider a mature live product generating €40m annually with stable DAU and slowing revenue growth. One fully operational Growth Cell costs approximately €700k annually once staffing, infrastructure, tooling, and operational overhead are included. The number is not insignificant, but neither is the surface area the cell operates against.

If that cell improves FTUE completion from 41% to 43%, D30 payer conversion from 5.0% to 5.8%, first-session purchase conversion from 2.7% to 3.1%, and overall ARPDAU by 4%, the economic effect becomes material very quickly.

A 4% ARPDAU improvement on a €40m annual revenue product produces approximately €1.6m in additional annualised revenue on its own. The increase in D30 payer conversion represents a 16% expansion in monetising users entering the mature economy, which produces downstream effects on retention and lifetime value that typically do not appear fully within the same reporting quarter. The FTUE improvement increases the efficiency of existing UA spend without requiring additional acquisition budget because a larger percentage of paid installs now reach monetisation surfaces successfully.

Under conservative assumptions, the Growth Cell recovers its annual operating cost within approximately five to six months.

The economics become more significant once multiple cells operate simultaneously across separate conversion points. A 2% uplift in FTUE completion combined with a 3% uplift in D7 retention, a 4% uplift in bundle conversion, and a 5% increase in event participation does not produce a simple additive effect. The gains compound because each improvement increases the throughput efficiency of the next monetisation surface in the player lifecycle.

This is the structural advantage engineered growth produces. The improvements are individually small enough to appear operationally insignificant and collectively large enough to materially alter the economics of the product.

On a €100m annual revenue title, a sustained 5% aggregate operational uplift produces approximately €5m in additional annual revenue before accounting for improved UA efficiency, retention extension, or increased LTV tolerance on paid acquisition.

The asymmetry is favourable because the downside risk is bounded while the upside compounds continuously. A poorly-performing Growth Cell costs less than a single failed feature initiative. A successful Growth Cell permanently improves the economic efficiency of systems already operating at scale.

This is the distinction between conventional LiveOps and engineered growth. Conventional LiveOps produces temporary engagement spikes tied to content releases. Engineered growth produces sustained improvements in the financial efficiency of acquisition, retention, and monetisation across the lifetime of the product.

Technical Foundation & Investment In Growth Architecture

Every operational property described so far depends on the same precondition: a client architecture and content delivery infrastructure built for ongoing dynamic content updates, rather than for the launch state.

Without this foundation, the growth cycle cannot run at speed, conversion-point teams cannot ship updates and new events independently, and the broader organisational structure has nothing to operate on. The technical foundation is not a supporting concern; it is the substrate on which engineered growth becomes possible.

The components are well understood and not exotic. Server-driven content allows tuning values, event parameters, economy variables, and progression curves to be adjusted without a binary release. OTA asset delivery allows new visual content, narrative content, and feature components to be deployed between updates. Remote configuration allows experiments to be defined, activated, targeted at specific cohorts, monitored, and rolled back through a configuration layer rather than through engineering tickets. None of these technologies are reserved for studios with nine-figure budgets. They are mature, documented, and within reach of any studio that decides to prioritise them. The choice not to build them is the issue.

Western studios optimise for rapid prototyping and early market testing because the funding environment rewards rapid proof points over architectural extensibility.

Building a server-driven, OTA-capable, remotely-configurable client takes months of engineering work that does not produce visible product progress, does not generate market-testable builds, and does not move the metrics that early-stage investors evaluate.

The rational response, given the incentives, is to ship a hard-coded client quickly, raise the next round on early performance numbers, and defer the architectural work until the game has revenue to justify it. The architectural work is then never done, because by the time the game has revenue, the team is operating against a roadmap of features that the existing client cannot easily support, and refactoring the foundation would require pausing feature development for a quarter or more.

The compounding cost is significant. Pre-production architectural decisions determine the studio’s LiveOps ceiling for the lifetime of the product. A studio that ships a hard-coded client on day one has bounded its own ability to respond to market signals from that day forward.

Every feature added to the client increases the cost of the refactor that never happens. Every quarter of LiveOps work against an inflexible foundation widens the gap between what the team wants to do and what the team can actually ship. The eventual response, observable across multiple Western mid-market studios, is a slow decline in event cadence, a creative library that becomes increasingly repetitive, and a feature roadmap that increasingly responds to internal capacity rather than to player behaviour.

Merge Mansion is the cleanest current illustration of this pattern. The studio reached the top of the merge-2 category early and held the position while its technical foundations were being refactored. During 2021, the period in which Merge Mansion was dominant, the studio’s content output was constrained by client architecture work that had to happen before more ambitious LiveOps was possible. Competitors that emerged later, primarily from China, shipped with server-driven foundations from day one and have since closed and overtaken Merge Mansion’s position in the category. The product quality of Merge Mansion remains high. The architectural decisions made in pre-production have nonetheless determined the trajectory of the game six years later.

The technical foundation argument is not a recommendation to refactor existing live products, which is rarely practical and almost never cost-effective at scale. It is a recommendation that studios building new products should treat client architecture and content delivery infrastructure as pre-production investments, comparable in priority to core engine choices and core gameplay loop design. The studios that win the next cycle will be the ones that made these investments before launch. The studios that did not will be operating, as Western studios are operating today, against ceilings they cannot easily raise.

Five Components of The Growth Mandate

The Growth Cell structure described in the previous section cannot function inside a studio whose organisational design has not adapted to support it. The cells need a broader mandate that establishes the conditions under which they operate, the authority they hold, and the boundaries within which they coordinate with the rest of the studio. Without this mandate, the cells exist on paper but cannot execute in practice; their work gets diluted by the same structural frictions that produced the plateau in the first place.

The mandate has five components. Each addresses a specific barrier that prevents engineered growth from being possible inside a conventional Western studio structure.

1. Dedicated team capacity. The Growth Cells are staffed by senior practitioners working full-time on their conversion point, not by people on loan from other teams. Part-time growth work produces part-time results because the iteration cycle requires continuous focus and the institutional knowledge of each cell builds up across weeks and months of dedicated attention. Studios that try to run growth as a side responsibility of the main team produce diluted versions of the work and rarely sustain the rhythm required for marginal gains to accumulate.

2. Co-ownership of the game roadmap with the GM, with defined executive escalation. The growth function and the GM are organisational peers, not subordinate and superior. Both have legitimate claims on the game’s roadmap, and both have legitimate authority to advocate for their priorities. When the two disagree, the dispute escalates to executive leadership for resolution rather than being decided unilaterally by either party.

This is the structural change most Western studios are most reluctant to make, because it explicitly creates a check on the GM’s authority over the product. The reluctance is rational from the GM’s perspective and incorrect from the studio’s perspective; without this structure, the growth function operates by persuasion alone and produces diluted work accordingly. Studios that want to keep the GM’s authority absolute while also expecting growth-system results are asking for something the organisational design cannot deliver.

3. Authority to execute time-bounded events outside the main feature cycle. Growth Cells ship their work on their own schedule through the configuration infrastructure described in the technical foundation section. They do not queue behind binary releases. They do not require sign-off from product management or from the GM for events within their conversion point’s scope.

The authority is bounded; the events are configuration changes against client capabilities that have already shipped, not net-new feature development. Within those bounds, the authority is genuine. A growth cell that has to negotiate every cohort-targeted event with the main team will produce nothing of consequence, because the cycle time of negotiation is longer than the cycle time of relevance.

4. Direct reporting line to executive leadership. The growth function reports to the CEO or COO, not to marketing and not to product. This sounds like a small structural detail and is in fact the change that determines whether the function has the standing to execute. A growth function reporting to marketing is a UA function with a broader title; the same function reporting to executive leadership is a peer to product with the structural authority to coordinate across functional boundaries.

The reporting line is the signal of the function’s mandate, and the signal is read accurately by everyone in the organisation. Where it sits on the org chart determines whether other functions treat its requests as priorities or as suggestions.

5. Authority over the technical infrastructure that growth depends on. The configuration platform, the attribution stack, the dashboarding capacity, and the engineering capacity to maintain and extend all three sit within the growth function rather than within general engineering.

Growth that depends on the central engineering team’s roadmap to ship its own instruments will not operate at speed; the cells have to own their own tools. This is the second-most-resisted of the five components because it requires moving engineering headcount out of a central function and into one that the central engineering leadership may not perceive as deserving of it.

The trade is worth making. A growth function without ownership of its instruments is permanently throttled by engineering capacity it does not control.

The function as cross-functional synapsis, not as replacement

A natural reading of the five components is that they constitute a power grab by the growth function at the expense of existing teams. They do not. The product team continues to own the game’s vision and the main feature roadmap. The GM continues to lead the game’s strategic direction. The central engineering team continues to own the core technical platform. The growth function does not absorb these roles; it integrates within them, with a defined remit and a defined set of authorities that complement rather than displace.

The metaphor that captures what the function actually does is synapsis. In a nervous system, the synapse is the connection that lets independent neurons act as a coherent network. The growth function plays the same role inside the studio. The existing teams remain intact and continue to do the work they were built to do. What the growth function adds is the linkage that allows their separate work to operate as one system rather than as parallel workstreams that occasionally coordinate in meetings.

In practical terms this means the growth function is in constant contact with every other team in the studio. It pulls insights from data engineering, hypothesises with product, designs with feature teams, briefs UA creative, and feeds learnings back into all of them. It is not a vertical silo; it is a horizontal layer that runs across the studio’s existing verticals and makes them faster, sharper, and more responsive than they could be operating independently.

The shift from distributed responsibility to unified accountability

The deeper change the five components produce is in how accountability for player outcomes flows through the organisation.

In the conventional model, every team is partly responsible for player outcomes and no team is fully accountable. Product is responsible for the game being good. UA is responsible for installs. LiveOps is responsible for engagement. Each team can defend its work in isolation while the overall trajectory drifts. When revenue declines, no single function can be held responsible because every function did what it was asked to do. The accountability is distributed across so many roles that it effectively disappears.

In the engineered model, the growth function is fully accountable for player outcomes within the scope of its conversion points. If FTUE completion drops, the cell responsible for FTUE is accountable. If day-one return weakens, the cell responsible for retention onboarding is accountable. The accountability is concentrated, the visibility is high, and the people holding the responsibility have the authority and the tools to act on it.

This is the change that most reliably distinguishes studios that grow from studios that plateau. Distributed responsibility produces collective shrugging. Unified accountability produces continuous improvement. The difference is not in the talent of the individuals; it is in the structural clarity about who owns what, and who has the authority to act when something needs to move.

Application to Mature Products

The argument so far applies most directly to new product development, where the technical foundations and organisational structure can be designed for engineered growth from the start. Mature products present a more challenging case. The technical foundation may already be locked in. The team structure may have ossified around the launch-phase roles. The senior leadership may have built their careers on the strategy that worked for the first eighteen months. None of this makes the structural argument less true; it makes the application more difficult.

The first task is recognising that the mature-phase plateau is what’s happening. The diagnostic signals are consistent across studios and products.

Revenue has been flat or slowly declining for several consecutive quarters, despite the team continuing to ship features and run events at the same cadence as before. UA spend produces diminishing returns; the campaigns that delivered installs at acceptable CPIs eighteen months ago now deliver fewer installs at higher costs, and the cohorts coming in convert and retain at lower rates than the launch cohorts did.

The event calendar has settled into a predictable rhythm and player engagement with events has flattened. Players who used to peak during themed events now treat them as background activity. The creative library is cycling through variants of campaigns that no longer outperform their predecessors. New creative tests produce results within noise rather than meaningful uplift. The team’s response to these signals is typically to propose more of the same: more features, more events, more creative variants, more UA budget. None of it changes the trajectory.

The incremental responses fail because they treat the plateau as a content problem when it is actually a structural one. Additional UA spend against the existing creative library produces additional installs of the same low-value cohorts the existing creative is built to attract. Additional features shipped through the existing roadmap produce additional content for the same flattened engagement curve. Additional events run through the existing LiveOps function produce additional repetition of the same patterns.

The studio is doing more of the work that produced the plateau and expecting a different outcome. The underlying issue is not that the studio is doing too little of the conventional work; it is that the conventional work has reached the limit of what it can produce against the existing player base. A different category of work is required.

Cohort analysis identifying current high-value player profiles. The personas the studio worked with at launch are no longer accurate. The current high-value cohorts have different motivations, different play patterns, and different content preferences than the launch-era cohorts did.

The work is to re-segment the active player base by behavioural and monetisation patterns, identify which cohorts are actually driving present-day revenue, and understand what those cohorts want from the game now.

This is foundational; every other component depends on knowing who the actual current players are rather than designing for who the studio remembers them being.

Install-source pattern work informing creative direction. The audiences arriving in the game now are different from the audiences that arrived at launch, often because the install sources themselves have shifted. Visual analysis of the top current install sources frequently reveals patterns the studio is not addressing. Players are arriving from games with specific interaction grammars, from creative campaigns with specific themes, from store positioning aimed at specific demographics.

The studio’s existing creative is built against the audience it imagined at launch; the audience that actually arrives needs creative built against who they are. This work typically produces the fastest measurable uplift because it addresses the acquisition funnel directly.

Daily mechanics rebuilt against current monetisation data. The daily appointment mechanics that worked at launch were designed against the launch cohorts. The current high-spending cohorts have different motivations and respond to different mechanics. Rebuilding daily content against current data, rather than against the assumptions the launch design was based on, often produces step-changes in engagement among the players who actually generate the revenue.

The work is unglamorous and rarely produces the headline of a major feature release, but it moves the numbers that matter.

Economy components create demand for items the high-spending cohort presently values. Mature game economies accumulate distortions. Players hoard items they used to find scarce. They min-max around monetisation pinch points the original design did not anticipate. The bundles on sale offer items the current high-spending cohort already has enough of.

Intervening on economy balance means reevaluating and redesigning what highest-value cohorts are being asked to buy, against an analysis of what they actually need and want right now. The optimisation is often small in scope (a new item category, a reconfigured bundle structure, a revised progression sink) and large in revenue effect.

These optimisations are not large features. None of them require building new core systems. All of them require the operational structure described in the previous sections: dedicated Growth Cells with the authority and tools to identify the opportunity, design the work, ship it through the configuration infrastructure, run the integrated campaign, and measure the result.

A studio that lacks this structure can theoretically execute similar optimisations but in practice will produce diluted versions of them on the cadence of the main release cycle, by which point the analysis the optimisation was based on is already stale.

The cost asymmetry of acting versus not acting is significant and is rarely calculated explicitly. Continued operation under the existing structure produces, with high confidence, another year or two of revenue plateau followed by slow decline.

The decline does not self-arrest because the structural conditions that produced it continue to operate. Structural updates, in the form described above, produces a return to measurable growth within two to four quarters with reasonable confidence, provided the studio is willing to make the organisational changes that any operational intervention requires.

The cost of development is substantial but bounded; the cost of the alternative is unbounded, because nothing about the existing trajectory contains the seeds of its own reversal.

The reluctance to act on this analysis is also predictable because it determines whether the analysis ever turns into action.

The existing structure was built by people who are still in the studio, often in senior positions. The proposed change implies that their structure has reached the limit of what it can produce. This is hard to hear and harder to accept, particularly for the people whose authority over the existing structure is part of their professional identity.

The framing matters significantly. The change is not a critique of the existing structure or the people who built it; it is a recognition that the structure was correct for an earlier phase of the product, and that a different phase now requires a different structure. Studios whose leadership can internalise this framing tend to make the change. Studios whose leadership reads the framing as a critique resist the change and continue to manage the decline.

The rationalisations for inaction are familiar. The team needs stability. The market conditions are difficult. The next big feature will turn things around. The new UA agency will produce different results. Investors will not understand a restructure. These are all true in some sense and all wrong in the sense that matters. They describe the difficulty of acting; they do not address the cost of not acting. The studios that grow are the ones whose leadership can hold both facts in mind simultaneously and act on the second one.

Forward View

A forward view in this context is not a forecast about which studios will succeed or which products will dominate. It is a description of what the structural argument made in this paper looks like when applied to a real studio over a real timeline. The work described in the previous sections is not theoretical. It can begin in the next quarter, take shape over the next year, and produce a structurally different studio within 12 months. This section describes how.

The first step is an honest diagnosis of current operations. The questions below are designed to locate where the studio actually is, not where the leadership team imagines it is.

  • Where does the growth function report on the org chart, and is that reporting line the CEO or COO, or is it marketing?
  • From the moment signals or growth opportunities are identified in the data, how many weeks pass before an event responding to that signal is live in the game?
  • How many concurrent experiments are currently running against the live product, and who can name them without checking?
  • Can a cohort-targeted event be configured and activated without a binary release, or does every event require an engineering push?
  • When a specific conversion point underperforms over the course of a quarter, is there a single named person accountable for that outcome?
  • What percentage of senior product time is spent on the main feature roadmap, and what percentage is spent on growth work specifically?

If the honest answers to three or more of these questions reveal conventional thinking around growth, the studio’s revenue trajectory will continue to deteriorate, and the cost of the eventual layoffs will be higher the longer it is deferred.

In the first ninety days, the work is assessment and proof. Three specific moves produce useful signal without requiring a full restructure. Audit the client architecture, documenting what requires a binary release versus what can be configured remotely; the gap between the two lists is the studio’s current LiveOps ceiling. Inventory the analytics and dashboarding capacity, identifying which decisions the existing instruments support and which decisions are being made on intuition. Identify two or three conversion points where measurable improvement is possible inside the current structure; these become the early proof points for the larger change.

Operational changes should begin as soon as possible. The highest-leverage move is to stand one Growth Cell up against one carefully chosen conversion point. Not five cells, not a full restructure, but one team given the technical infrastructure, the dedicated capacity, and the executive mandate to operate with autonomy.

The choice of conversion point matters: it should be one where the data is available, the levers are accessible, and the political resistance to a new team with new authority is manageable. The cell’s first-year job is to produce both operational results and organisational evidence. The results demonstrate the model works on the studio’s specific product; the evidence builds the political capital for the broader restructure that follows.

Within two to three years, the full model is in place. Multiple Growth Cells operate in parallel across the major conversion points of the studio’s portfolio. The growth function sits at peer level with product, reporting directly to executive leadership. Technical foundations have been rebuilt where the original architecture made the operational tempo impossible. Accountability for player outcomes has shifted from distributed across functions to concentrated in the cells that own specific surfaces. New products launched in this period are designed with growth systems integrated rather than retrofitted later. The operational tempo is visible from outside: higher event cadence, more responsive creative, sustained chart positions, mature products that keep growing rather than plateauing.

The harder honesty is that most studios reading this will not do the work. Change is difficult, slow, politically expensive, and threatens the authority of senior people who built the existing structure. Studios will make incremental adjustments instead, hire growth leaders into unchanged structures, and discover within eighteen months that the adjustments did not produce the expected results. The studios that do make the change will be in the minority, and they will define what the next decade of Western mobile F2P looks like.

The studios that begin the assessment now will be operating differently within twelve months and will be unrecognisable within thirty-six. The studios that defer it will, in the same period, watch the gap to their competitors widen by a margin they will not be able to close without paying significantly more than it would have cost to begin today.

Conclusion

The central argument of this essay is that growth in mobile F2P is an engineered organisational outcome, not a market-determined one. The dominant Western studio model is structurally configured to produce competent live services that cannot grow systematically. The gap between Western and Eastern operators is observable in operational terms long before it shows up in revenue. The gap is durable because the capabilities that produce engineered growth are difficult to assemble retroactively and require structural commitments that most Western studios have not made.

Studios operating under the conventional model pay for it in flat revenue, declining creative effectiveness, slower response to market signals, and senior growth hires who depart within eighteen months because the structure they were hired into made their work impossible. The cost is rarely calculated explicitly because the comparison is not made against an explicit alternative; studios assume their performance is bounded by market conditions rather than by their own design.

Restructuring becomes possible when leadership accepts that the existing structure was correct for an earlier phase and a different phase now requires a different structure. The restructuring is not a critique of the past; it is a recognition that the present requires different choices than the past did. It becomes necessary when the alternative is honestly compared to the trajectory of competitors who have already made the change. The comparison is uncomfortable, which is part of why it is rarely made.

The studios that make the comparison and act on it will define the next decade of Western mobile F2P. The studios that do not will be remembered, if at all, for the games they made in the prior decade.
